# Messaging Implications: AI Proposal Builder for Custom Kitchen Makers

*Skill: startup-positioning | Generated: 2026-05-01*

This document is the bridge from positioning to copy. It tells the copywriter, marketer, and salesperson which words to use, in what order, and why.

---

## Messaging Hierarchy

What to communicate first, second, third. Every piece of copy should follow this priority:

1. **The dollarizable pain (Theme A).** *Stop charging for proposals. Stop chasing tire-kickers.* This is what gets the maker to read past the headline.
2. **The artifact (the maker outcome).** *A quote-ready brief in your inbox, in your trade language.* What they actually receive.
3. **The mechanism (one-line how).** *AI interviews the homeowner in plain language; you receive a structured spec.*
4. **The category label.** *AI proposal builder for custom kitchen makers.*
5. **The differentiator (Theme C / integrations).** *Plugs into Houzz Pro, 2020 DesignFLEX, Builder Prime — your funnel, not a marketplace.*
6. **Trust scaffold.** *AI-assisted intake, human-reviewed brief. EU AI Act compliant. Disclosed every conversation.*

Not every piece of copy needs all six levels. But when present, they should appear in this order. The most common mistake will be leading with #4 (the category) before #1 (the pain) — resist that.

## Category Label

**Always use:** "AI Proposal Builder for Custom Kitchen Makers"

Use this exact phrase consistently across:
- Website (hero subhead, footer, About page)
- LinkedIn company page tagline
- Twitter/X bio
- App marketplace listings (Houzz Pro, Builder Prime, JobTread, etc.)
- KBIS / KBB Birmingham booth signage and printed materials
- Investor decks (one-liner, intro slide, cover)
- Cold-email signature

**Avoid these alternative phrasings** unless deliberately testing:
- "AI chatbot for kitchen designers" — drifts to Tidio pricing anchor
- "AI lead qualification for kitchen studios" — too generic, buries the artifact
- "Kitchen design AI" — confuses with RoomGPT/Spacely
- "Smart intake form" — sounds like Typeform
- "AI assistant for cabinetmakers" — too vague

**When the audience needs a one-step-broader frame** (e.g., a generalist investor or a non-kitchen contractor reading at random):
*"AI client-discovery and proposal automation for trades — purpose-built for custom kitchen and cabinet studios."*

## Value Anchor

**"What you used to charge $400 for as a design fee."**

This is NOT the category label. The category describes WHAT it is (an AI proposal builder). The value anchor describes WHAT IT'S WORTH.

Where to use:
- Body copy on the homepage
- Pricing-page comparison block
- Sales-call objection responses
- Investor pitch slide on willingness-to-pay

Where NOT to use:
- Headlines
- Category descriptions
- Taglines (unless carefully tested — risk of making the product sound *cheaper* than the design fee, devaluing the craft)

Example body copy:
> A custom kitchen studio used to charge $300–$500 to produce an initial proposal — partly to recover discovery time, partly to filter tire-kickers. The fee scared off real buyers and ranked design-fee shops at the bottom of industry revenue. We replace the filter, not the proposal: your AI does the discovery interview, you review the brief, and you decide who's worth a meeting.

## Customer Language vs. Category Language

Customers describe the problem in one set of words. Buyers and analysts categorize the product in another. Use customer verbs in headlines and category nouns in supporting copy.

| Customer says (use as verbs in headlines / hooks) | Category says (use as nouns / outcomes) |
|---|---|
| "stop being ghosted" | structured brief delivery |
| "stop wasting hours on tire-kickers" | qualified-lead automation |
| "stop charging for proposals" | proposal automation |
| "show up to the first meeting prepared" | client-discovery automation |
| "lead quality is terrible" | lead qualification |
| "the homeowner doesn't know what they want" | requirements capture / intake |
| "Pinterest board they have to decode" | multimodal input handling |
| "Houzz sends me spam" | proprietary funnel ownership |
| "I want a brief, not a render" | structured spec output |
| "feels like another Houzz" | integration-first SaaS |

**Bridge in copy:** use customer verbs with category nouns.

Example:
> *"Stop being ghosted by leads that were never going to buy. Get a structured brief on every real homeowner — in your inbox, in your trade language."*

(Customer-verb headline + category-noun outcome.)

## Words to Use / Avoid

| Use | Instead of | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Brief** | "summary," "lead," "form output" | "Brief" is what makers and architects already say. Anchors against the design-fee replacement story. |
| **Quote-ready** | "AI-priced," "estimate-ready" | Quote-ready ≠ priced. Implies the maker still does the quoting; we just make it possible. Avoids the "AI cannot quote" objection. |
| **Trade language** | "industry jargon," "technical specs" | Respects the maker's craft; signals vertical specificity. |
| **Studio / shop / maker** | "contractor," "designer" | "Contractor" pulls toward HVAC; "designer" pulls toward Houzz/Decorilla. "Studio" and "maker" are how custom kitchen folks describe themselves. |
| **Intake / discovery** | "qualifying," "screening" | "Qualifying" sounds salesy and BANT-y; "discovery" is the word makers already use for the first call. |
| **Embed / branded** | "white-label," "co-brand" | "Branded on your site" is plain language; "white-label" is jargon. |
| **Integrates with / plugs into** | "API-first," "open" | Plain English. Names the actual integrations (Houzz Pro, 2020 DesignFLEX, Builder Prime). |
| **Custom kitchen / built-in / bespoke** | "renovation," "remodel" | Specifies the segment; avoids being lumped with general home-services intake (Avoca, LeadTruffle territory). |
| **Homeowner** | "consumer," "user," "client" | "Homeowner" is what makers say. "Client" comes after the proposal is signed. |
| **AI assistant** | "AI agent," "AI scoper," "autonomous AI" | Lower-key positioning manages the 18%-trust-AI-to-act objection. |
| **"Reduces stress" / "feel prepared"** | "reduces friction" | Verbatim from Sweeten and HCB Kitchens reviews. Customer language wins. |

**Avoid entirely** (these phrases trigger the wrong category or wrong objection):
- "AI chatbot" — pulls pricing to $50/mo
- "AI-generated kitchen" — triggers "wonky AI" misread
- "AI-priced quote" — triggers contractor objection ("AI can't quote")
- "Replaces your designer" — anti-craft narrative; sales-killer
- "Automates the design process" — overclaims; design isn't automated
- "All-in-one platform" — Houzz Pro owns this lane; we'd lose the comparison
- "Streamline your workflow" — generic SaaS-speak; says nothing
- "Boost conversion" — generic SaaS-speak; says nothing
- "Empower your business" — generic SaaS-speak; says nothing

## Social Proof Guidelines

**Do not fabricate or pre-write social proof.** Only use quotes from published, verifiable sources, or from real design-partner customers who consent to attribution. Placeholder quotes damage credibility more than no quotes at all.

If no real social proof exists yet (likely at launch):
- **Omit the section entirely.** A blank social-proof slot is better than a fake one. Visitors who notice fake testimonials will distrust everything.
- Replace it with measurable outcomes from design-partner pilots: *"In our first 10 design-partner studios, briefs reduced first-call duration by an average of X minutes"* (only if you actually measure this).
- Use industry-data social proof until customer quotes exist: *"Houzz Pro carries 1.03/5 BBB. Custom kitchen makers deserve better intake."* (Cited public data, not customer testimonial.)

When you have real customer quotes, look for these patterns (which research suggests will resonate most):
- Maker quotes about hours saved per lead
- Maker quotes about brief quality vs. Houzz lead quality
- Homeowner quotes about reduced anxiety / preparation
- Integration-specific quotes ("works with my 2020 / my Builder Prime")

Highest-credibility quotes name the alternative explicitly: *"I cancelled Houzz Pro Lead Generation"* > *"I got more leads."*

## Trust Scaffold (must appear on every page)

The 18%-trust-AI-to-act data and the "wonky AI" cluster mean the product carries inherited skepticism. Every page must include:

1. **AI disclosure.** "You're talking to an AI assistant" — visible at conversation start, in every transcript, in the footer.
2. **Human-review beat.** "Your maker reviews this brief before responding." — communicates "draft for review," not "autonomous agent."
3. **Brief, not quote disclaimer.** "This is a brief. Your maker prepares the quote." — pre-empts the AI-can't-quote objection.
4. **Data-handling line.** "Your photos and conversation stay private; export at any time; EU data residency available." — addresses GDPR / EU AI Act baseline.

These are hygiene, not differentiators. Don't market on them; bake them in.

## Naming the Risk in Public

Two narrative risks are worth pre-empting on the website itself, not just in sales calls:

1. **The Modsy comparison.** Investors and savvy makers will ask. Have a one-paragraph answer:
   > *"Modsy was a $72M direct-to-consumer interior-design service. We're a B2B SaaS for makers. The homeowner uses our chat because their maker invited them — not because we marketed to them. The maker pays us; we never compete with them."*

2. **The "AI cannot quote" objection.** Address head-on:
   > *"We don't quote. We brief. The AI captures everything your quoting tool needs — measurements, hardware, appliances, budget band — and you produce the price. The AI never tells the homeowner a number."*

Putting these on the FAQ page in the maker's first session removes them as silent objections.

## Freemium / Pricing Positioning *(if applicable)*

The launch pricing model isn't locked yet, but research suggests three viable shapes. Pick one:

**Option A — Per-qualified-brief (consumption pricing)**
- Free: 5 briefs/month or 14-day trial
- Paid: $X per qualified brief (where "qualified" = brief passes minimum-spec threshold)
- Story: "Pay for what works."
- Best for: makers with variable lead flow.

**Option B — Per-seat / per-studio (subscription pricing)**
- Tier 1: $199/mo — 1 studio, basic integrations
- Tier 2: $399/mo — multi-location, full integrations
- Tier 3: Custom — multi-brand, white-label
- Story: "Predictable monthly cost."
- Best for: makers who want budget certainty.

**Option C — Hybrid (subscription + per-brief)**
- $99/mo base + $X per brief
- Story: "Floor + variable."
- Best for: capturing both small and growing shops.

**Anchor recommendation:** Option B at launch. Anchors against Houzz Pro ($149–249/mo) and JobTread ($329/mo), which are the two prospect comparisons. Per-brief pricing risks educating prospects on the "cost per lead" math before they trust the brief quality.

If freemium: never make the free tier feel "incomplete" to push premium. The free tier should deliver full value within its scope. Premium should add a different scope (multi-location, white-label, advanced integrations), not "more of the same."

## Red Flags

- **Pricing-anchor drift.** If sales calls or PR articles refer to the product as a "chatbot" or "AI assistant," prospects will benchmark at $50/mo. Treat this as a P0 messaging discipline issue. Brief every interview, every demo, every cold email with the Houzz Pro anchor.
- **PandaDoc misread.** "Proposal builder" overlaps doc-template tools. Mitigation: always pair with the discovery-interview demo, never lead with the PDF output.
- **Modsy comparison.** Investors will draw it. Pre-empt aggressively.
- **"Replaces my craft" narrative.** Cabinet makers are wary of AI replacing skill. Use "assistant," "intake," "draft for review" — never "automates the design."
- **Fabricated social proof.** First instinct will be to write placeholder testimonials. Resist. Real quotes earn trust; fake ones obliterate it.

## Yellow Flags

- **"Stop charging for proposals" tagline** can read as "we devalue your design work." Always pair with the second-half framing or risk offending the very designers it's targeting.
- **"AI assistant" framing** that manages the trust objection may also undersell the product. Watch the line: too apologetic-sounding kills the perceived value.
- **Channel-specific one-liners may drift** if multiple writers ship copy. Centralize a one-pager glossary that maps audience → tagline → differentiator and require sales/marketing to use it.
- **Trust-scaffold language** (AI disclosure, "draft for review") is critical but should not bury the value prop. Hierarchy matters: pain → outcome → trust scaffold, in that order.
- **Pricing model is unlocked.** Option B (subscription) is recommended but unvalidated with real prospects — first 5 design-partner conversations should pressure-test willingness-to-pay before locking in the public price page.

## Data Gaps

- **No customer-language testing** of the headlines / taglines / one-liners with actual makers. The verbatims used are sourced from public reviews/forums, not interview transcripts of the ICP.
- **No A/B test data** on cold-email subject lines, paid-ad copy, or landing-page hero variants. Run within 90 days of launch.
- **No social-proof quotes** at launch — recommend tracking measurable outcomes (briefs/month, conversion delta, hours saved per qualified project) from the first 10 design partners and using those numbers as proof.
- **No translation work** for non-English markets. The current copy assumes English-language launch (US/UK/IE). German, Italian, French, Spanish customer-language maps are a Phase-2 deliverable and *not* a translation of this English copy — they need their own customer-language research.
